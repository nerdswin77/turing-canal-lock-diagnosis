## What this models

The setting is a waterway authority with a canal of 11 or 12 locks in a row, each with one chamber, on a busy day: 120 barges and pleasure craft run through it in 13 to 28 hours. The stored plan is the nominal canal: every keeper serves the vessels first come, first served, every valve is clear, every chamber has its full length, every crew works at the documented speed. Loggers at 3 or 4 of the locks note the minute at which each vessel left the chamber. Their clocks are off by their own amounts, they show only multiples of 1, 2 or 5 minutes, and they miss a vessel now and then. In the real canal exactly 5 locks work differently (the authority orders a full audit of a canal when its count of locks flagged out of order reaches 5, and the count does not name them), and every keeper holds a part-filled chamber for the same hidden number of minutes in the hope of more vessels, which saves lockages and water.

Each departure is something a waterway operator sees. A keeper who does not serve first come, first served does so for a reason: `FIRST_FIT` fills the chamber and saves a lockage; `LARGEST` passes the large commercial barges first, which are the hardest to fit in later; `SMALLEST` loads the small craft that moor quickest, so the lockage starts sooner; `LIFO` takes the vessel that moored last, because vessels that wait rafted two or three abreast in a short lay-by lie with the last arrival outermost, and the outermost can cast off without moving the others. A partly blocked valve is silt or debris in a filling culvert, a shortened chamber is a damaged gate leaf or sill, a slow crew is a short-handed or inexperienced one.

The numbers are those of a main waterway. Chambers are 180 to 210 m long and 12.5 to 16 m wide, the lift is 2.5 to 9 m, and the valve area is chosen so that a full chamber fills in 9 to 21 minutes by the orifice law in the statement (valve areas of 2.5 to 13.3 square metres). Large barges are 110 m long, medium barges 85 m, small barges 55 m and pleasure craft 12 m; mooring takes 1 to 4 minutes and leaving 1 to 3 minutes per vessel, and a large barge needs 12 to 36 minutes between two locks. The waiting time is 0 to 18 minutes, in steps of 2.

Departures are drawn as follows: the kind has a chance of 30, 25, 20 and 25 percent for a loading rule, a blocked valve, a shortened chamber and a slow crew, and the strength or rule is then drawn with equal chances among those that change the log at that lock. A loading rule or a shortened chamber is placed only where it moves at least 12 readings by more than 30 minutes; where no rule does, the lock gets a blocked valve or a slow crew instead. That is why loading rules are not equally frequent in the private canals (see Verification).

## Difficulty

Think of a canal with 11 or 12 locks in a row and 120 vessels. Loggers sit at only 3 or 4 locks. What they report is rough: each clock is off by its own amount, times are rounded to 1, 2 or 5 minutes, the readings are one sorted list with no vessel numbers, and up to three exits were never logged. You have to name the 5 locks that behave differently from the nominal canal and exactly how (a different loading rule, a partly blocked valve at one of five strengths, a shortened chamber at one of four lengths, or a slow crew at one of five extra times), and the hidden waiting time that every lock allows before it sends off a part-filled chamber.

A blocked valve stretches every lockage and every empty trip of the lock, but the fill time rounds up to whole minutes, so a small blockage can change nothing at one lock and a minute at another. A slow crew costs time in proportion to the number of vessels in the chamber. A shortened chamber and a loading rule only matter while the queue would fill the chamber. The loading rules differ in which vessel they leave behind, and what leaves one lock reaches the next after the sailing time. Most locks have no logger, so a fault there shows up only as late exits further along, mixed with other delays. A solver that does not know how a lockage is built cannot tell a slow crew from a blocked valve at a lock that mostly serves one vessel at a time.

With no vessel numbers you must line up two sorted lists while allowing for missing entries and an unknown clock offset, and one missing reading shifts every later entry by one place. One wrong departure changes which vessels share a chamber, so a guess that is partly right can look worse than an empty guess. Each lock can be in 19 states.

The obvious first attempt is coordinate descent on a nearest-value error, with random restarts and the waiting time as one more coordinate. Of the 24 candidate canals seen by the final filters, that search found the exact answer for none. All 18 private canals must be exactly right, so a single wrong value fails the run, and the solver has 900 seconds.

## Reference solution

`solution/solve.sh` copies `solution/solver.py` to `/root/results/solver.py`, runs it on the development campaign and compares the result with the development answer key.

For each canal, the solver simulates the canal exactly for a guessed set of departures and a guessed waiting time. It uses whole minutes and the same event order as the statement, and it runs on two worker processes. The main search is a beam search that always explains the earliest unexplained reading first. For every guess it simulates the canal, sorts the predicted exits at each logger, and counts how many readings, from the start of the log, the prediction reproduces exactly at the best clock offset, skipping the few exits that were not logged. The beam starts from one guess for each waiting time and tries every single change (another waiting time, any of the 18 departures of a lock, or returning a lock to nominal). It keeps the guesses that reproduce most of the log from the start, and among equal ones those with fewer departures. When one guess reproduces every reading, it drops each departure that the readings do not need, so the answer has as few departures as possible.

If that search stalls, the solver falls back to a second search on a misfit that lines up each sorted prediction with the sorted readings (a matched pair costs its difference, capped at 30 minutes, and skipping an entry costs 30), with pairs and triples of changes and random restarts.

The truth does not come from this solution. `tests/provenance/generate_data.py` draws the canals, the departures, the waiting time and the lost readings, simulates each canal with `lock_model.py`, and writes the readings and `tests/cases/*/truth.json`. `select_lines.py` applies the two filters described below.

## Verification

The verifier runs in a separate environment with no network. It holds three private campaigns of six canals each, with their true answers, as checksummed archives (`tests/cases/H*/H*_input.tar.gz`). The isolation setup (an unprivileged solver account, read-only staged inputs, resource limits, an isolation probe and a live `/dev/shm` watcher) is adapted from the `navigation-sensor-calibration` task in this repository (Apache License 2.0); the rest is specific to this task.

The solver runs once per campaign with the command line from the instructions, a 900-second limit, 2 CPUs and 4 GB, and an output directory as its only place to write (`/tmp` is not writable for the solver account). The watcher uses inotify: directories, other names and more than 1000 created entries fail the run, and if inotify cannot be set up the run stops with an environment error. The solver can import only the standard library, numpy and scipy. It starts with hash seed 0, one numerical thread and global generators seeded with 0. `diagnosis.json` is checked with `lstat` and must be a regular file with a single name, so a link cannot point the root-owned verifier at a private file. Six tests per campaign check the format and each answer exactly.

The reference needs 228 to 241 seconds per campaign on 2 CPUs and peaks at 41 MB with 6 tasks, so 900 seconds are 3.7 times the slowest campaign, and 4 GB, 64 tasks and 16 MB leave a wide margin.

The 18 private canals have 90 departures (33 loading rules, 19 blocked valves, 21 shortened chambers, 17 slow crews), 9 different waiting times and 28 lost readings. The loading rules are FIRST_FIT 4, LARGEST 10, LIFO 12 and SMALLEST 7; four slots (A2, B1, C2, C4) were drawn with a required rule so that none is missing. `tests/provenance/check_stated.py` checks every bound the instruction states on all 20 shipped canals, and the generator asserts them before it writes a canal.

A canal is only generated if every single-change alternative (dropping a departure, giving a lock another one, moving one to another lock, or changing the waiting time) raises the full misfit by at least 150, which is five readings displaced by the 30-minute cap, and if the best clock offset of every logger is the true one and beats every other by at least 12.

Two more filters were used and they do bias the set. A canal was dropped if the nearest-value descent in `tests/provenance/descent_baseline.py` (110 seconds) found its exact answer, or if the first search of the reference solver did not explain every reading within 120 seconds. The second check compares nothing with the true answer: the search sees by itself whether it has explained the whole log. A replay of the whole drawing kept the same 18 canals, byte for byte: the 6 dropped candidates all stopped with part of the log unexplained, and none finished with a wrong answer. Still, the reference is tested on canals that its first stage can explain in time, so 18 of 18 is not an independent test. Both filters apply to the development canals too: S1 and S2 pass them (first stage exact in 101 and 65 seconds, descent misses both). Of 24 private candidates seen by the final filters, 18 were kept and 6 were dropped by the reference check, none by the descent. The independent evidence is that on 12 further canals drawn without either filter the full reference was exact on all 12.

## Contested conventions

The statement fixes each of these. For every one, this is what was chosen, what else could have been chosen, and why it was not.

- **Order within one minute (finish, arrive, decide).** The alternative is to let a freed chamber be used only from the next minute. That would leave a chamber idle while a vessel is waiting, which no lock keeper does.
- **The waiting time counts from the later of the idle start and the first arrival.** The alternative is to count from the first arrival only. A chamber that has been busy would then start an unfilled lockage the moment it became free, and the waiting time could not be told apart from zero.
- **A chamber is full when something is left behind or less than the smallest class fits.** The alternative is to call a chamber full only when it has no free metre. A full-looking chamber would then wait for a vessel that can never fit.
- **The fill time rounds up after the valve blockage is applied.** Rounding the nominal time first and then scaling would hide small blockages at locks with short fill times. Rounding once, after scaling, lets a blockage show whenever it crosses a minute boundary, and no fill time of any canal is within 0.01 minutes of a whole number, so double-precision details cannot decide it.
- **A shortened chamber does not change the fill time.** The alternative is to scale the chamber area. The water volume of a shortened chamber would then change the valve law, and a gate fault could not be told apart from a valve fault.
- **A slow crew adds time per vessel, both on entering and on leaving.** The alternative is to add it once per lockage. That would look like a longer fill time, and the crew fault could not be told apart from a valve fault at a lock that is nearly always used by one vessel.
- **The vessels leave in the loading order.** The alternative is to let them leave in arrival order. The loading rule would then change nothing for vessels that are taken together, and only the choice of who is taken would matter.
- **Ties in a loading rule go to the earlier arrival, then to the lower vessel number.** Random ties would make the true schedule impossible to reproduce. These keys always exist and always settle the tie.
- **The clock offset is added before rounding down to the resolution.** The offset belongs to the clock and the resolution to the display, so the display rounds what the clock shows.
- **A lost reading leaves no trace.** There is no placeholder in the list, because a real log that missed a vessel does not mark the gap.
- **Exactly five departures.** The audit rule of the authority fixes the count at the moment a canal is sent for audit, so every canal has exactly five. A random count was rejected because it would not match that rule, and the count would then be an extra unknown.
- **Only rules a keeper can apply at the lock.** The five loading rules rank the queue by arrival minute or by vessel length, which a keeper sees at the berth, and each has an operating reason (see What this models). A ranking by something no keeper knows, such as the destination of the vessel, was not used.
